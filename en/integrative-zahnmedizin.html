2015 Integrative Dentistry Agenda

By Christoph Arlom, published in: GZM – Practice and Science, Vol. 14, No. 2/2009.

Integrative medical concepts are becoming established within university structures. This article provides food for thought on integrative dentistry.

Cultural studies scholars distinguish between folk medicine, therapies provided by alternative practitioners, and complementary medicine. The latter advocates a synthesis of medically proven methods—and is therefore not at odds with conventional, pathogenetically oriented medicine. Complementary medical approaches complement conventional medicine primarily through a salutogenic perspective: they emphasize autoregulation, the body’s self-healing abilities, and patients’ personal responsibility.

In Switzerland, “complementary medicine” is a precisely defined term ever since this branch of treatment became part of official medicine through a referendum and a chair in complementary medicine was established at the University of Bern. Here, the term “complementary medicine” encompasses “homeopathy, anthroposophic medicine, neural therapy, Traditional Chinese Medicine, and acupuncture” (9).

Complementary Systemic Dentistry

Systemic models of thought (14) form the basis of complementary medical methods. They offer more comprehensive conceptual possibilities for prevention, diagnosis, and therapy than linear-causal models. From an integrative dentistry perspective, material incompatibilities arising from fillings, prosthetic restorations, and implants; subacute and chronic inflammation in the jaw region; and subliminal stresses on the body’s statics—stemming from functional disharmonies in occlusion and the temporomandibular joint—can establish pathophysiological links to symptomatologies and their syntropies, thereby influencing the dynamic biopsycho -eco-social (6) system of the human being.

An individual’s constitution is decisive for the development of health disturbances or manifest diseases caused by psychological stressors and toxic agents. Many practicing dentists expand upon classically taught dentistry in their daily practice to include complementary medicine diagnostics and integrative therapy. The orofacial system must be regarded as part of the organism from an anatomical (i.e., macro- and micromorphological) and physiological perspective, taking into account aspects of regulatory biology. Dental meridian diagnostics—whether performed using instruments or through consultation—is part of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), while regulatory medicine offers an alternative interpretation of established physiology. Methods from Traditional European Medicine, psychosocial competencies, and environmental dental medicine analytics also require integration (1).

Thus, integrating complementary medical history-taking, diagnosis, and therapy into the conventional university curriculum enables dentists to gain a broader perspective on complex, chronic clinical pictures, prevention, and a renewed appreciation of dentistry as a specialty within human medicine. The foundation of any therapy is always conventional dental conservative, surgical, and prosthetic rehabilitation.

Integrative medicine

Germany’s first “Chair of Naturopathy and Integrative Medicine” was established in 2004 as part of a pilot program by the state of North Rhine-Westphalia: the Department of Internal Medicine V, Naturopathy, and integrative medicine at the Essen-Mitte Academic Teaching Hospital, under the direction of Prof. Dr. Gustav Dobos. The goal of the department is the scientific evaluation of methods from naturopathy and Traditional Chinese Medicine (5).

The Outpatient Clinic for Prevention and Integrative Medicine (CHAMP) opened at the Charité in Berlin in 2007. CHAMP’s concept involves treating patients using both conventional and complementary medical methods while consistently evaluating treatment success through scientific means (3).

The Concept of Integrative Medicine: What Does Integrative Medicine Mean?

Professor Dobos’s group defines the term “integrative medicine” as follows: We understand the term “medicine” metaphorically and interpret it as “multilingualism.” The integrative physician makes use of a meaningful interplay of various medical approaches from conventionally proven medicine (the term “conventional medicine” is deliberately avoided, as it was originally understood to be discriminatory), applied naturopathy, and mind-body medicine. At the same time, they incorporate active, behavior-based approaches (for dentists, these may include individualized preventive care and nutritional counseling—author’s note) to manage disease. He addresses the patient’s individual situation without prejudice. He makes the decision regarding the appropriate therapeutic option by considering the relevant scientific evidence for the respective therapeutic procedure (external evidence) and based on his own experience as a physician (internal evidence) (4).

Integrative Perspective: The Example of Endodontics

This internal evidence is of decisive importance in dentistry with regard to endodontics (2) and thus to regulatory medicine, meridian-specific, and psychoneuroendocrine-immunological (11) relationships. According to Haag (2005), 8 million teeth undergo endodontic treatment in Germany each year. Consequently, after ten years, every citizen carries a potential site of bacterial infection within their body.

“Referring to a landmark article by Wu et al. published in the renowned International Endodontic Journal on the modest results of infection control in root canal treatments according to the internationally recognized gold standard of endodontic specialists, the editor-in-chief of the journal Endodontic Practice writes in the editorial of the May 2006 issue, in essence: As an endodontist, it is extremely depressing to have to accept the fact that worldwide, more than half (other sources cite up to 85% (7, 12, 13)) of all root fillings are teeming with bacteria and that there is virtually an epidemic of unresolved apical bone infections.” (8)

Follow-up examinations focusing on regulatory and environmental medicine, as well as immune and meridian-specific monitoring (2), and risk education take place only to a limited extent. The issue concerns the responsibility toward the patient to consider an odontogenic focal infection as a possible cause when conventionally diagnosed chronic disease courses manifest in the fields of orthopedics, neurology, ENT, and others. For it is not the irritation that has occurred (in this case, root canal treatment as a procedure providing external evidence) that is decisive, but rather the internally evident, immanent, individual systemic reaction.

Conclusion

All knowledge is only understandable in the context of the time in which it is acquired and processed. Therefore, conventional perspectives appear within the same framework as complementary—and thus integrative—ones. From this emerges a complex picture that yields a new convention in biomedical knowledge (10)—a biological oral medicine as a fusion of state-of-the-art dentistry and the art of healing?
