Controversial Foundations of Biological Oral Medicine

By Heinz Spranger

All knowledge can only be understood in the context of the time in which it is acquired and processed. Therefore, conventional perspectives appear within the same framework as complementary—and thus integrative—ones. From this emerges a complex picture that gives rise to a new convention in biomedical knowledge, one that also incorporates the latest findings.

Human ecology shares similarities (and differences) with both ecology and the nano- and femto-scales, as found in “geophysics” and in the “physics of small things.” It would seem surprising if rules that have been found in other fields of knowledge to differ only in their order of magnitude—but to be otherwise identical—were not to apply to physiology and biological medicine simply because they do not fit the current state of scientific convention. In 1956, Pischinger, and in 1997, Heine—influenced by a changing understanding of morphology—assigned a linear-causal abstraction to the concept of the cell in cellular morphology, because the cell, in the conventional medical sense, had been stylized into a model for practical reasons. If we apply the life-sustaining principle of the triad—capillaries, ground substance (as a matrix and mediator of correspondence), and cells and tissues—to the consideration of complex medicine, we come close to a systemic perspective. This systemic perspective is necessary because, in biological medicine, many different clinical pictures can be hidden behind the same set of symptoms. Therefore, one should approach the body’s intrinsic system via the internal body surface—that is, “from oral to anal” and from “rhinal to pulmonary”—because the morphological and physiological patterns of the mucous membranes are very similar to one another.

From a general medical perspective, it is important to note that all mucous membranes are capable of allowing certain substances to pass through or of acting as semipermeable barriers:

The thin, non-keratinized mucosa of the sublingual region allows for the absorption of medications, which thus enter the systemic circulation immediately [Löning 1984]. This therapeutically desirable absorption is also a decisive factor for toxins typical of environmental exposure and those relevant to oncology. Consequently, nearly 60–80% of all oral cavity tumors are found in this region, which accounts for only 16–20% of the oral mucosa [Lehmann 1991]. The same phenomenon compromises the body’s integrity via periodontal lesions [Spranger 1980].

Resistance barriers are structured in stages: A dense bacterial flora (as a “biofilm” consisting of individual bacteria, bacterial clusters, and bacterial colonies) on the surface of all mucous membranes counteracts an influx of foreign pathogens into the spaces lined by the mucosa, preventing immediate colonization and thus local metabolism (with its toxic end products) by transient foreign pathogens. Between their attachment sites, they compartmentalize both effluxing and infiltrating fluids. Furthermore, they possess their own chemical composition, which (unlike certain foreign pathogens) is accepted by the body. This naturally also applies to all “exposed” body surfaces, i.e., external, internal, traumatic, and iatrogenic wound surfaces. This is the level of colonization resistance provided by an intact site biofilm, which allows for both agglutination and exchange as well as local breach. Predisposing factors for such a breach include, on the one hand, microtraces of subbacterial elements—which also include viruses and toxins—and, on the other hand, desquamation due to the turnover of the barrier layers. The role of non-microbial exotoxins has been greatly underestimated to date. This also includes topical therapeutic agents (even “simple plant extracts”), because some disinfectant solutions act selectively on and within the biofilm and could thus also weaken colonization resistance. In doing so, they may also destroy certain immune modulators.

The mucous membranes possess a dense, tightly packed pattern of cells that—increasingly so toward the deeper layers—exhibit such effective membrane functions that, even with minimal folding, they allow only very little toxin passage. Chemically, proteoglycans, hyaladherins, and lectin-binding proteins come to the fore—the “homing” superfamily among the intercellular cross-linkers. Directly at the cell membrane, the ectodomain has a site vulnerable to proteolytic attack. If it is detached—either due to functional defects in the cell membranes or a severe external toxin surge—toxin resistance is thereby reduced; the barrier can collapse. It is possible that this effect occurs much earlier as a result of etiologically heterologous dystrophic epithelialization. Such conditions then lead to erosions and ulcers.

The subepithelial barrier—that is, the ground substance lying closest to the surface—is rich in free cells that have a dendritic structure and character, are motile, and are capable of phagocytosis. They are found “from oral to anal” and were referred to as “lining cells” in their earliest descriptions [Heine & Spranger 1995]. In the context of cellular pathology, they would be classified as macrophages based on their chemotactic organization. In biological medicine, they are considered information mediators for humoral defense. They are thus energetically responsible for cellular resistance.

The reticular layer of the stroma is richly vascularized; together with the epithelial basement membrane, it serves not only as the starting point for diffusion and the nourishment of the epithelium but also as the structural foundation of the humoral resistance barrier. It does not make a significant distinction between carrier substances (c-proteins), (plasma) cells, and free immunoglobulins (IgE is responsible for mast cell apoptosis).

From this perspective, it becomes clear that between the biofilm and its structural organization, via its ground substance, lie extracellular information detectors and mediators that can function as the taut strings of a rapidly responding system. Heine (1997) even attributes resonant capabilities to them, which explains the easily observable high speed in the chain of events.

The physiology of the mucous membranes will therefore have to address the possibilities of immune correspondence between the functional human-ecological conditions and the complex morphological conditions in order to do justice to the biological perspective.

Functional transition to the ground substance

Conventional medicine, which serves as a didactic model, is based on “cellular pathology”—a concept developed by Dr. Ludwig Rudolf Carl Virchow, who served as professor of pathology and surgery in Berlin from 1855 to 1902—which places the cell at the center of pathological processes and attributes disease to changes in the cell. This pathology served as the most important foundation for medicine. It inspired the great scientific era in medicine. However, it failed—particularly in its later developments—to take into account the relationships with the nervous and vascular systems. Furthermore, various direction- and speed-related functions in the vertebrate body cannot be accounted for. Thus, despite thorough consideration of all neural and vascular connections in the body, it remains unclear how certain “immediate phenomena” are to be explained.

Exemplary Physiology of the Ground Substance

Pischinger described the “system of ground regulation.” He became the founder of fine-tissue histochemistry and provided explanations for communication phenomena in connective tissue structures throughout the entire organism. The basic regulatory system he named comprises a network of capillaries, the intercellular fibroblast and fibrocyt milieu, cell membranes, and the autonomic nervous system’s terminal structures, all of which are connected to lymphatic tissues and fluids. The biochemical foundations are proteoglycans and structural glycoproteins, which structure and permeate the macromorphological environment in a mosaic-like pattern [Pischinger 1975].

Biological Ground Substance and Strings

The structuring of this ground substance depends on a dynamic equilibrium that provides a molecular sieve function. After being released into the extracellular space, the synthesized backbone of the sugar polymers is processed by enzymes such as glycosyltransferases, ecto- and endoglycolidases.

The network-like structure of the ground substance can further be explained by its function as “strings,” which we understand as mediators of communication. Every function requires energy. Every form of energy requires both sites of origin and sites of action. In cellular pathology, these are the cells with their ADP and ATP systems. In ground substance physiology, these are the ground substance “strings” and tunnel structures arising from the spatial arrangement of the biopolymers of the sugar chains. Here, hydrophobic “inner strings” and hydrophilic “outer strings”—with diameters ranging from 5 to 80 micrometers, which are attributed to the matrisomes—play a role. Matrisomes consist of four macromolecules: proteoglycans/glycosaminoglycans, structural glycoproteins, cross-linking glycoproteins, and transiently bound proteins. The latter include cytokines, growth factors, hormones, enolases, and other active substances with varying degrees of efficacy and localization.

Bioenergetics

Energy is a measure, a physical quantity representing changes in matter.

A key reason why energy and matter began to take on a semantic life of their own was Einstein’s formula E=mc². What this actually states is that if matter—consisting of protons, neutrons, and electrons—is completely converted into radiation, i.e., photons, this finer form of matter possesses an immense amount of energy. Photons are also a form of matter, since, for example, as they travel through space, they are attracted and deflected by the gravitational fields of planets and stars, or they can collide with other particles or be struck by them.

When considering biological—that is, living—forms in terms of their energy, one would not expect to find only one type of energy, as is often associated with discussions of “life energy” [Hommel 2008].

Drawing on other fields of knowledge, it is also evident that energy often has a material carrier—for example, an electron, a photon, a moving or heated object, and so on—and that this need not always be the case. For example, a gravitational or electric field possesses a certain amount of energy without consisting of particulate matter. From an energetic perspective, processes in biological systems involve the presence of potential and kinetic energy. Embryonic stem cells, for example, are cells with very high energy, since they possess the inherent ability to transform into any other cell type.

It is known from the physical sciences that a system that no longer exhibits any order, direction, or structure no longer possesses energy. This is characteristic of the heat death of a system—the completely disordered motion of elementary particles. For biological measurements, it can be inferred that the more order, direction, and structure a system exhibits, the higher its energy.

It must be clarified whether tunnels in the ground substance are capable of releasing energy through flow between hydrophobic and hydrophilic regions. According to Heine’s interpretation of Pitha’s [1983] results, mass transport within the ground substance is facilitated in a surprisingly simple manner. The possibility of ring-closing in the sugar components of PG/GAGs gives rise to tunnel-like structures measuring approximately 5–80 nm, which—like cyclodextrins—have a hydrophobic inner wall and a hydrophilic outer wall wetted with water. Proteins, lipids, immunoglobulins, cytokines, and even hormones are taken up into the tunnel lumen by displacing water and transported further. In the process, the water is flushed out of the lumen and shifts into the tissue space between the ground substance and the cells.

As soon as all chemical processes that occur in the body’s water compartment have been completed within the tunnel lumen, the outward flow of water decreases. This phenomenon causes molecular chains to be compressed. The particles previously dispersed in the water collapse together. With a relatively small mass, this contraction can still come to a halt. The greater the mass, the smaller and denser the clump becomes. This is how immune energy condenses. The nature of the action emanating from this energy cannot be predicted because different particles emerge in complex patterns. In this respect, this transport pathway is singular. At the stage of singularity, therefore, no laws apply, because at different times there are different combinations of unknown magnitude. At the event horizon—the boundary of the tunnel—only a steady-state equilibrium with tissue water is to be expected. It is expected that these tunnels have a rate of expansion and collapse that depends on their contents. If their OH bonds on the outer surface change very rapidly, one can also expect very rapid actions. Each of these tunnels thus emits clusters of reactions that are subject to the complex chemistry of the ground substance. Following this line of thought, it follows that a functioning tunnel within a network structure of the ground substance can be large or small. The speed at which it transmits information—for example, regarding immune competence—is independent of its size or length.

If one could calculate the mass available at the tunnel’s opening and its density, this would provide a point of reference for the speed of exchange with the water above it. This would allow one to compare the speed of impulses traveling through the body and thus compare information from the fundamental substance with that of the neural pathways.

However, if particles are trapped within this tunnel system, “slagging” can occur, which prevents further transport and exchange of substances. The more pathways that are “slagged,” the more “detours” information must take; consequently, the slower the capacity for action and reaction within the ground substance.

A tunnel with a cross-sectional area of 10 micrometers and a flow velocity higher than that of blood or lymph could also produce electron-proton pairs. This would preserve electric charges, angular momentum, and magnetic fields.

Immune Correspondence

The semantic definition encompasses a biological functional unit (of vertebrates) with specific reaction processes at the humoral and cellular levels. This ensures an individual structure (integrity and individuality). In contrast, the immune system of invertebrates lacks specificity in its defense mechanisms. This biological functional unit, the “immune system,” is highly dependent on the quality, degree, and quantity of information transfer—for example, between antigen haptens and carrier molecules on the one hand and the antibody system on the other. The immunological individual structure must therefore be organized systemically. It thus possesses structural-biological characteristics that can most likely be found in the fundamental system. The likely candidates are the described network-like proteoglycans and structural glycoproteins, which function as information conduits.

Natural passive immunity

Maternal immunoglobulin is transferred to the fetus via the placenta (through IgG placental passage) or through colostrum, thereby providing the fetus with immunity as temporary protection before the fetal organism learns to mount its own immune response. In the interim, the passively transferred antibodies are metabolized or excreted by the fetus. This phase is generally brief, though it carries significant risks. This is particularly true for premature infants, and especially for those with extremely low birth weight. It is known that these infants often come from mothers who exhibit lifestyle-related or otherwise acquired complex syndromes involving pathological expressions on the body’s internal surfaces. We have published findings on this phenomenon in mothers with a complex of periodontitis symptoms, consistent with the existing literature [Spranger 2000].

Physiological active immunity

Based on morphological knowledge of the external and internal body surfaces, it can be inferred that the resistance of tissue surfaces to colonization stems from the dense biofilm. It prevents the attachment of foreign pathogens. On the other hand, it has its own metabolism, which naturally also produces antigens. These antigens constantly stimulate the immune system; they ensure the system’s responsiveness and thus also contribute to immune correspondence. Of morphological significance is the fact that the biofilm rests on a surface of dense cell layers, so that the passage of toxins into deeper layers is at least impeded. This toxin resistance is an important structural-biological barrier mechanism. Other barriers serve different functions. In the subepithelial connective tissue lies the cellular resistance barrier, which consists of antigen-presenting cells. These are cells of the mononuclear phagocyte system. While resting monocytes develop into activated macrophages in the subepithelial papillary space following antigen contact, they exhibit their typical effector mechanisms of cellular immunity.

In addition to these, as Heine, Spranger, and Schaeg demonstrated in 1993, there are resident cell types with accessory functions in the stratum papillare of the connective tissue. They are not “professional” phagocytes, but they do bind antigens definitively without modifying them. Many are characterized by numerous star-shaped processes and are therefore grouped under the term “membrane-protruding dendritic cells.”

Dendritic cells are found as “Langerhans cells” in the epidermis and in the mucosal epithelium, interdigitating (ALSO “in line” with lining cells).

Dendritic cells migrate to T-cell-dependent regions of the secondary lymphoid organs and, as follicular cells, to B-cell-dependent regions of the lymphoid organs. In the epidermis and epithelium, they continue to perform barrier functions; in lymphoid regions, they perform contour functions, through which they temporarily replace missing linkages as information mediators. Thus, they act as mediators within the proteoglycan and structural glycoprotein network; in conjunction with the semantically loosely defined “slagging” of the ground substance system—for example, in individuals with substance dependencies—they maintain immune information for a very long time, as Heine & Spranger demonstrated in 1995.

Unlike macrophages, dendritic cells do not shed the immunogenic molecules (peptide/MHC complexes) once they have formed on the cell surface. This is an essential prerequisite for their ability to induce an immune response in the lymph node against the antigens they have taken up in the periphery. The description of the structure and function of dendritic cells is attributed to Steinman (Rockefeller University, New York), who received the Robert Koch Prize in 1999 for his publications. With his presentation and the conclusions drawn from it, he laid the foundation for the development of tumor vaccines.

Through this rapid transport, they perform an adjuvant function. Incidentally, in the classic medical vaccination regimen, adjuvants are administered together with the antigens [Bundschuh, Schneeweis & Bräuer 1991]. This effectively enhances the immune response. If dendritic cells are loaded with foreign antigens and then injected, for example, into laboratory animals, no adjuvant is necessary to elicit a strong primary immune response (= immunization). This observation led to dendritic cells being referred to as “nature’s adjuvant.” The morphological appearance of dendritic cells reveals how the veil-like membrane projections enormously increase the cell surface area of a mature dendritic cell. This not only allows for the binding of many antigens but also facilitates contact with numerous T lymphocytes, thereby creating the conditions—through a sort of continuous “screening” of the lymphocytes passing through the lymph nodes—to identify those that possess the appropriate receptors for the antigen in question.

The function of the dendritic cell ends once information carriers are fully bound to its impressively large cell surface. Once this membrane is completely occupied, the biological information carrier becomes redundant in other areas of the periphery because it can no longer act as an adjuvant collector at its molecular sensors. The fate of the cell within the ground substance is straightforward: The cell membrane consists of two lipid layers with embedded proteins. Viewed in detail, the inner and outer layers are each composed of a different assortment of building blocks. Under the destructive influence of apoptosis, this order begins to falter. Phospholipids such as phosphatidylserine, which normally remain on the inner side, migrate outward. Proteins and carbohydrates also reorganize. This causes the cell membrane to rupture; a lipid molecule from the inner cell membrane migrates outward, making the cell visibly capable of apoptosis—and thus serving as a biological signal for cellular death. The cell debris is cleared away by mononuclear phagocytes. This has been demonstrated in the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, which has only a single protein acting as a receptor molecule. This protein resembles a membrane protein that captures defective lipoproteins and removes them from function. It is hypothesized that the signaling molecule is phosphatidylserine (Zhou 2002).

Terminology: Immunomodulation

Active substances of various origins influence an immune response, on the one hand by enhancing suppressive properties—even to the point of inducing tolerance to toxicological substances and antigens—and on the other hand by acting as immunopotentiators (Keller 1987). With these modern insights, the immune system—previously known exclusively as a defense system—not only gains unusual functional modules but also exhibits self-similarities (i.e., inequalities) in form and function that in the past constituted traditional schemas. An understanding of biological medicine, which is based on these principles, must include an understanding of fractals (fractus = broken, used to describe things that appear to be irregular). Fractals may exhibit approximate or statistical similarities; in any case, they have a form that is independent of geometric predictability. The definition of “fractal” in biology, like the definition of “life,” is a serious description of the complexity that must be grasped.

The framework of Biological Oral Medicine encompasses not only a previously unconventional approach to physiological and pathological processes but also a revised canon governing the interplay between environmental influences and modulated responses within the human ecological context. It is therefore worthwhile to examine the details of these perspectives in light of the conventional understanding of salutology, nosology, and semiotics. The systemic picture that emerges from this also brings innovations to anamnesis and catamnesis.
